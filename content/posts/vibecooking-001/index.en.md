I've been freelancing for three months. At first lunch was the same as office life. Open the delivery app, scroll, pick something, wait. Nothing wrong with it. I'd been doing it for years.

The shift wasn't sudden. First I noticed the monthly delivery bill added up fast. One person ordering has to meet minimums plus packing and delivery fees, and a decent meal runs $6-8. At month's end it wasn't pocket change. Then I noticed how heavy the oil and salt were. I'd eat, then crash. Paying money to feel worse.

Two thoughts stacked up: save some money, eat cleaner. The "just order it" option slid down the priority list.

I cook most days now. Not out of passion. I just found the barrier was lower than I thought. This series is what I've been collecting: how to use AI to adjust cooking techniques, how to let the oven/steamer/rice cooker watch the heat for me, and how to design meals that batch-prepare on weekends and reheat quickly on weekdays.

But today starts with the simplest thing I can make fast. Nothing to prep or plan. Just fridge staples and the ritual of turning on the stove.

## Tomatoes and Eggs, Ginger Greens

Two dishes, three ingredients from any grocery store. Three eggs, two tomatoes (about 200-250g), half a bag of baby bok choy (about 120g), and a knob of ginger.

Start the rice first if you're having it. Modern rice doesn't need rinsing for sand, just a quick wash. Slice the tomatoes and put them in a bowl with a pinch of salt. This matters. I'll explain why later. Slice the ginger into thin strips and split into two portions: one for the tomatoes (yes, ginger with tomatoes works), one for the greens.

While the tomatoes sit, wash the bok choy and crack the eggs. If you like visible egg white in the final dish, beat just the yolks loose without over-mixing. Add a pinch of salt to the eggs.

**Step 1: Scramble the eggs.** Drain the bok choy and get the pan hot, medium-high for about a minute, then a spoonful of rapeseed oil. Add scallions if you have them. Half a minute later, pour in the eggs and turn to medium heat. After about 20 seconds the bottom sets. Roll the egg mass with a spatula and flip. About 90 seconds total, the oil is absorbed and the eggs are half-set. Scoop them out.

**Step 2: Cook the tomatoes.** Quick rinse of the pan, same routine: heat, oil, ginger strips until fragrant. Dump in the tomatoes with all their juices. High heat for a few stirs, then down to medium-low. Press the tomato chunks with the spatula as they cook. Break them down as much as you can. About 3-5 minutes. Watch the tomato state, not the clock.

**Step 3: Combine.** Add the eggs back, break them into pieces, mix with the tomatoes. Chunk size is personal preference. Some want big pieces, some want them fine. About a minute, then off the heat. Sprinkle salt to taste, plate.

**Step 4: Stir-fry the greens.** Same pattern: high heat, oil, ginger strips, then the greens. Stir-fry for 2-4 minutes until the thickest stems go soft. Off the heat, salt, done.

Two dishes in under half an hour, timed with the rice cooker.

All the seasoning: two spoonfuls of low-erucic-acid rapeseed oil, about 2g of salt, a knob of ginger, optionally some scallions. That's it. I asked AI to estimate the nutrition: three eggs are about 20g protein, plus fiber and vitamins from the greens. A quarter serving of Greek yogurt stays for the afternoon snack. Total: around 300-330 kcal. No refined-carb spike or heavy oil, and way cleaner than delivery.

## Why Salt the Tomatoes First

I didn't know this trick before. I asked AI out of curiosity, and it explained from a food chemistry angle: **osmosis**.

Salt creates a concentration gradient on the cut surfaces. Water inside the tomato cells, along with sugars and flavor compounds, gets pulled out. When this liquid hits the hot pan, it's already outside the cells, so it concentrates immediately instead of waiting for cell walls to break down from heat. The salt also softens the fruit tissue, making it easier to crush into sauce without long simmering. And a small amount of salt makes the sweetness more pronounced.

That's the VibeCooking pattern: one question, one practical insight.
