I've been freelancing for three months. At first, lunch was the same as office life. Open the delivery app, scroll, pick something, wait. Nothing wrong with it. I'd been doing it for years.

The shift wasn't sudden. First I noticed the monthly delivery bill added up fast. One person ordering has to meet minimum order thresholds plus packaging and delivery fees, and a decent meal runs $6-$8. By the end of the month, that wasn't pocket change. Then I noticed how heavy the oil and salt were. I'd finish eating, only to hit an immediate post-meal slump. Paying money just to feel sluggish felt like a raw deal.

Two thoughts stacked up: save some money, eat cleaner. The "just order it" option slid down the priority list.

I cook most days now. Not from passion, exactly. I just found the barrier was lower than I thought. This series documents what I've been testing: how to use AI to adjust cooking techniques, how to let the oven/steamer/rice cooker watch the heat for me, and how to design meals that I batch-prepare on weekends and reheat quickly on weekdays.

But today I'm starting with the simplest thing I can make fast. Nothing to prep or plan. Just fridge staples and the ritual of turning on the stove.

## Tomatoes and Eggs, Ginger Greens

Two dishes, three main ingredients from any grocery store. Three eggs, two tomatoes (about 200-250 g), half a bag of baby bok choy (about 120 g), plus a knob of ginger.

Start the rice first if you're pairing your meal with carbs. Modern rice is clean enough that a quick rinse is all it needs. There's usually no sand to pick out. Wash and slice the tomatoes, then put them in a bowl with a pinch of salt. This matters. I'll explain why later. Slice the ginger into thin strips and split into two portions: one for the tomatoes (yes, ginger with tomatoes works), one for the greens.

While the tomatoes sit, wash the bok choy and crack the eggs. If you prefer distinct yellow-and-white streaks in the cooked eggs, beat the eggs loosely without over-mixing. Add a pinch of salt to the eggs.

**Step 1: Scramble the eggs**. Drain the bok choy and preheat the pan over high heat for about a minute, then add a teaspoon of rapeseed oil. Add scallions if you have them. Half a minute later, pour in the eggs and turn to medium heat. Once the bottom sets after about 20 seconds, gently push and fold the egg curds with a spatula. After about 90 seconds in total, the oil is absorbed and the eggs are half-set. Scoop them out.

**Step 2: Cook the tomatoes**. Quick rinse of the pan, same routine: heat, oil, julienned ginger until fragrant. Dump in the tomatoes with all their juices. High heat for a few stirs, then down to medium-low. Press down on the tomato chunks with your spatula to break them into a rich sauce. About 3-5 minutes. Watch the tomato state, not the clock.

**Step 3: Combine**. Add the eggs back, break them into pieces, mix with the tomatoes. Chunk size is personal preference. Some want big pieces, some want small pieces. About a minute, then off the heat. Sprinkle salt to taste, plate.

**Step 4: Stir-fry the greens**. Same pattern: high heat, oil, julienned ginger, then the greens. Stir-fry for 2-4 minutes until the thickest stems become tender-crisp. Off the heat, salt, done.

Two dishes in under half an hour, timed with the rice cooker.

Total oil and seasoning used: two teaspoons (about 8 grams) of rapeseed oil, about 2 g of salt, a knob of ginger, optionally some scallions. That's it. I asked AI to estimate the nutrition: three eggs are about 20 g protein, plus fiber and vitamins from the greens. A small side portion of Greek yogurt is saved for an afternoon snack. Total: around 340 kcal. There's no refined-carb spike or heavy oil, so it's much cleaner than delivery.

## Why Salt the Tomatoes First

I didn't know this trick before. I asked AI out of curiosity, and it explained it from a food chemistry angle: **osmosis**.

Salt creates a concentration gradient across the cut surfaces, drawing out water along with natural sugars and glutamates. When this liquid hits the hot pan, it evaporates and concentrates instantly, without needing prolonged heat to break down the cell walls. Salt also softens the tomato's pectin structure. That makes it effortless to crush the tomatoes into a thick sauce without long simmering. A pinch of salt also enhances flavor contrast, making the natural sweet-and-sour notes pop.

That's the VibeCooking pattern: one question, one practical insight.
